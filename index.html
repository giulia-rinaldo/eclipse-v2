A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth and blocks some of the Sun's light. From the place where the Moon's shadow falls, the Sun looks partly or completely covered.

Four kinds of solar eclipses

Every eclipse in the record is one of four kinds and what separates them is a few thousand kilometres: whether the tip of the Moon’s shadow reaches the ground, stops short of it, or misses the Earth entirely.

Seen from the side, the Moon's shadow is a cone with a weaker halo around it. The dark core is the umbra and the halo is the penumbra. Beyond the tip of the cone the shadow opens up again as the antumbra. Which of them touches Earth sets the type of eclipse.
The definitions are from the NASA's glossary.

In the 21st century alone the catalogue has eclipses: partial, annular, total and hybrid. Magnitude puts them on one scale. For total, annular and hybrid eclipses it is the Moon's apparent width divided by the Sun's: above 1 the Moon covers the Sun, below 1 a ring is left, and hybrids sit close to 1 and cross it along their path. For a partial eclipse it is the share of the Sun's width that is covered.

Considerations on the data

First, a duration is a measurement on the central line at greatest eclipse. For the 585 BCE eclipse NASA gives 6 minutes 4 seconds, measured in the North Atlantic. The armies fighting in Anatolia saw a different time and the catalogue can't say which.

Second, the catalogue records what happens and when, not why. It gives dates, types, magnitudes and durations and it does not explain the differences between one eclipse and another.

Third, the future is a prediction. NASA says the largest uncertainty in its predictions comes from changes in Earth's rotation, so the further away the date, the less certain the exact time.

Three smaller limits also apply. Dates before 15 October 1582 are in the Julian calendar and years are numbered astronomically: there is a year 0, and 585 BCE is written −584. The coordinates in the catalogue mark the point of greatest eclipse, not the whole path. And whether Thales predicted the 585 BCE eclipse is disputed; only its date is treated as established.
